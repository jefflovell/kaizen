I Want to Believe

What do The X-Files, The Iliad, and Saving Private Ryan have in common?

I’ve been fascinated by the possibility of extraterrestrial life for as long as I can remember.

I was the kid who watched The X-Files religiously when it aired in primetime. I spent my Scholastic Book Fair money on UFO books instead of posters and pencils. I grew up equally at home aboard the Enterprise and the Millennium Falcon, and I begged my parents to send me to Space Camp because becoming an astronaut seemed like the closest thing my generation had to becoming an explorer. That fascination never really left. It just grew up alongside me.

I eventually found myself in engineering and then machine learning, where curiosity became tempered by probability even as the universe somehow became stranger. I learned enough physics to appreciate how little we know, enough astronomy to understand how absurdly large the cosmos really is—causing me to forget my towel and suffer a few panic attacks over it—and enough statistics to become suspicious of certainty, especially my own.

I’ve also seen things in the sky that I still can’t explain. More than once, and not alone. Other people were standing beside me, looking at the same things. I still don’t know what we saw, and I’ve carried that uncertainty around for years because it feels more honest than replacing it with a convenient story. So if you came here expecting an argument for or against UFOs, I’m probably the wrong person to write it. I’m too interested, definitely biased, and increasingly comfortable admitting how much I don’t know.

So of course I had to see Spielberg's 49-years-in-the-making spiritual successor to Close Encounters of the Third Kind: Disclosure Day; and I must say, I was rather surprised. I expected to leave the theater thinking about aliens, maybe mashed potatoes. Instead, I found myself thinking about people and Spielberg's catalog.

Steven Spielberg has spent nearly fifty years making movies about first contact. Close Encounters of the Third Kind arrived in 1977, and almost half a century later he finds himself beneath the same sky, still asking questions about what happens when we encounter an intelligence unlike our own. This is also the guy who made Jaws, Hook, Indiana Jones, Jurassic Park, Minority Report, A.I., Catch Me If You Can, and Saving Private Ryan. He has made seven or eight alien movies, depending on how generously you define “alien,” and even sent Indiana Jones moonlighting as Fox Mulder, complete with a visit to Area 51, flying saucers, and little green men in bodybags. Hold on to your potatoes, Dr. Jones! The truth is out there...

At first glance, Spielberg's filmography looks like a garage sale. Aliens, dinosaurs, World War II, Neverland, archaeology, artificial intelligence, wire fraud, VR, state surveillance, and a big-ass shark do not appear to share much DNA. Somehow, though, they fit together like peanut butter and jelly; and upon further thought, I realized it's because Spielberg’s real subject has always been people. More specifically—confrontation with the unknown: who we are as individuals, how we organize ourselves as a society, and what happens to our moral certainty when reality refuses to behave as expected. He doesn't often paint a rosy picture of us, come to think of it.

Science fiction is an interesting genre because people tend to love it or hate it. There aren’t many people who feel tepid about Star Wars. But science fiction is well suited for societal analysis. It is, more or less, just technological fantasy. As Arthur C. Clarke once said, sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic. One might even go so far as to say that myth and fantasy are just the science fiction of antiquity. It bothers my wife deeply that there is no visible exhaust thrust on all those floating vehicles and that every planet seems to have the same atmosphere. It bothers her so deeply as a rational being that she can't get over it long enough to appreciate the story. However, she has no trouble suspending her disbelief when Lacey Chabert discovers the true meaning of Christmas for the thirty-seventh time with another hunky, wealthy, and conveniently single Midwesterner after returning home for the holidays. She also loves Sharknado, much to my chagrin. In her opinion, Star Wars is trying to be serious, whereas those movies understand that they are emotional junk food. I say it's the viewer, not the story. ¯\_(ツ)_/¯

The thing is, science fiction has never really depended on death stars, teleporters, giant sandworms, or whether the spaceships have proper exhaust. Those are sets and settings. The genre uses the impossible to give us enough distance to become honest about society. Just like Lacey and the local hunk-with-a-heart-of-gold are there to help us understand that connection and meaning create more wealth than material goods. An alien is easier to examine than a political opponent. A robot gives us a psychologically safer way to think about consciousness. Vampires let us approach mortality indirectly. A galaxy far, far away can reveal more about the neighborhood across the street than a story set there ever could.

This is nothing new. Aristotle postulated that the mythos or plot was the most important aspect of the story and broke it into three sections. Horace claimed five acts. Over the next 2000 years poets and philosophers argued over the finer points, rediscovered lost ideas, and debated the quality of the perfect story. Once you notice the similarities, it becomes difficult to unsee. I grew up reading everything I could get my hands on. Homer to Herbert. Corinthians and Creighton. Twain and Tolkien. Anne Rice and Ayn Rand; and the patterns are remarkably consistent. The costumes change while the questions remain the same. Genre is just another classifier to organize these stories.

The Iliad is remembered as the great poem of war, but Homer ends it with grief rather than conquest. Achilles finally meets Priam, the father of Hector, and for one fragile moment the greatest warrior in the world stops seeing the King of Troy. He sees another father mourning his son, and it breaks him.

The war does not disappear, and neither does their hatred. Empathy enters the chat and changes the meaning of everything around it. Patroclus’ death drove Achilles to seek out Hector in the first place, yet when Priam begs for his son’s body, Achilles can think only of his own father. I’ve always understood The Iliad as a story about humanity more than conquest. The horse and Achilles’ death aren’t even in the epic, while the sequel is a seemingly endless catalog of miseries suffered by Odysseus on the wine-dark sea for being a generally shitty human being and an insufferable asshole after sacking Troy. #TeamAjax.

Tolkien hides a similar question beneath orcs, wizards, and rings. The speciation and fantastic settings create space for criticism. People remember the battles and the Balrogs, but the fate of Middle-earth ultimately depends on mercy and second chances. Bilbo cannot bring himself to kill Gollum. Frodo later wishes that he had, until Gandalf reminds him that mercy stayed Bilbo’s hand and may still shape what follows. Nearly everyone believes they know exactly what kind of creature Gollum is, including Gollum himself, yet his desire for the Ring becomes the final instrument of Sauron’s destruction. Aragorn, Théoden, Faramir, and the armies gathered around them create the opportunity, but the discarded creature everyone had already classified finishes the job. The difference between Frodo and Sméagol turns out to be less a difference of kind than of time and circumstance.

Dune uses sandworms, prophecy, and the Kwisatz Haderach to examine colonialism, exploitation, demagogues, and messiahs. Paul unleashes the Fremen on the galaxy much as Shaddam IV wielded the Sardaukar or the British exploited...you know what, let's table that for later. The point is that nobody is the villain in their own story, though Paul comes close. Whether they appear as heroes of a revolution or agents of terror depends largely on where the observer is standing. Power changes hands while its logic survives. The oppressed gain power, reproduce the machinery that oppressed them, and construct a righteous story about why this time it is different. That social commentary was not subtle when Herbert wrote it, and in 2026 I’m not sure what to tell you if you still can’t see that it's not a story about space ships anymore than The Odyssey is a story about a boat.

Which brings me back to Spielberg and his apparent obsession with aliens, dinosaurs, war, Neverland, archaeology, fraud, and sharks. His stories repeatedly bring people into contact with something they have already decided how to classify, and then allow that encounter to destabilize the story they were telling about themselves.

You barely see the shark until the end of Jaws. Most of Amity treats it as either a public-relations problem or a trophy waiting to be killed and stuffed. Quint is the only person who truly respects what it is, though his respect has curdled into obsession. In Jurassic Park, living creatures become an engineering accomplishment, intellectual property, and a marketable attraction before anyone seriously considers the moral consequences of bringing them back. Dr. Malcolm’s objection is famous because it applies well beyond dinosaurs: everyone was so consumed by the possibility that they neglected the responsibility.

Peter Banning becomes Peter Pan only after he stops treating the Lost Boys as childish and recognizes that his adult certainty has become its own form of foolishness. Indiana Jones repeatedly encounters supernatural forces that his rational, academic mind rejects, and he survives only when he allows reality to be stranger than his model of it. In Saving Private Ryan, Captain Miller tries to preserve a small measure of decency inside the F.U.B.A.R. machinery of war. His private mission is to keep doing a little good so that the categories imposed by war—soldier, enemy, casualty, objective—do not entirely consume the people trapped inside them.

The settings differ, but the encounter is the same. Spielberg’s characters meet something profoundly outside their experience and must decide whether to approach it with curiosity, humility, fear, domination, or violence. His movies quietly return to one question: can we meet what is different without first deciding that it is less than us?

I think this is a remarkable point of reflection in our current age and a remarkable feat of Spielberg to have been so utterly consistent in his story-telling over his career. We have become extraordinarily good at labeling one another. We have a category for damned near everything and everyone, and soon enough we will probably be arguing about biological humans, augmented humans, artificial minds, and whatever comes after that.

To be fair, we may not have much choice. I spend enough time around machine learning to understand why classification exists. No intelligent system—biological or artificial—can process every encounter entirely from first principles. We build models, compress reality, and sort experience into categories because that is how intelligence scales. It is how we scale.

The danger begins when we forget that the model is only a model. As George Box said, All models are wrong. Some are useful.

Maps are useful because they simplify the land. Categories are useful because they simplify people. Trouble follows when the simplification becomes more authoritative than the thing it was meant to describe. A model can be useful, predictive, and internally coherent while still being catastrophically wrong about the individual standing in front of it. Anyone who has watched companies use algorithms to suppress wages, manipulate prices, or turn human desperation into a revenue signal should understand that the distance between classification and exploitation can become very small, very quickly.

The characters who move us in these stories are usually the ones willing to revise their categories when reality contradicts them. Achilles looks at Priam and remembers his own father. Bilbo allows Gollum to remain more complicated than the creature he appears to be. Captain Miller tries to keep hold of the person he was before the uniform and the mission reduced everyone to roles. They update the story they had already decided to tell. In machine-learning terms, they retrain the model.

That willingness feels conspicuously absent from modern life. We’ve become remarkably efficient at diagnosing everyone else and organizing the world into smaller and smaller boxes. “Who are you?” has displaced “Who am I?” and, more importantly, “Who am I in the presence of you?” Demagogues and petty dictators concern themselves with the stranger. Big thinkers and genuine leaders concern themselves with the self.

Maybe that’s why the ending of Disclosure Day stayed with me. The alien says one word: “Listen.” The internet seems to think it’s a cliffhanger. I choose to think it’s Spielberg's mic drop.

Listening creates a rare interval in which we suspend our model of another consciousness long enough to let it become real again. Empathy does not require agreement, pity, acceptance, or approval. It asks only that we admit another person contains more than our first classification of them. Even the Devil has an advocate.

We could all use a little more willingness to believe that another consciousness contains multitudes. Every enduring story about “the other” eventually turns the mirror around. The alien reveals our assumptions. The vampire exposes our relationship with mortality. The enemy tests our capacity for mercy. The monster illuminates our instinct for hierarchy and order in a chaotic world. These stories endure because they examine our willingness—or unwillingness—to update the story we have already decided to tell.

They are asking whether we would recognize our own petty motivations if they were standing directly in front of us. Three thousand years after Homer, I still don’t think we know the answer, but the question still feels worth masticating. I'm also just a science nerd with a theater degree who's into aliens so... maybe it ain't that deep. ¯\_(ツ)_/¯
