The Fuzzy Logic of Nolan’s Odyssey

Part 3 · 13 min read

The Horse Was Made of Trust

What survives a victory when winning becomes the only rule?

Part 1 Prologue Part 2 Taxidermy Part 3 The Horse

Hot take: The Horse was a dick move.

It was a magnificent dick of a move, certainly. Breathtaking. Peerless. Unforgettable. History’s most successful phishing attack. Ten years of siege couldn’t breach the walls of Troy, so Odysseus left an executable on the beach marked THOUGHTS AND PRAYERS 🙏 and waited for Grandpa Priam to double-click it.

He clicked it. Game Over.

In our inherited heroic tradition, the Horse is Odysseus’ masterpiece. It is the ultimate expression of mētis: cunning intelligence, tradecraft, and the ability to solve through adaptation what strength alone could never overcome. Achilles could kill anything he could reach. Ajax could hold the line against anything put in front of him. Odysseus looked at the line, the walls, the rulebook, the players, the board itself, and decided to play a different game without telling anybody.

That’s why I have always liked him. That’s why most people like him even if they don’t know that’s why they like him.

Odysseus is Homer’s proof that intellect can defeat force. The Greeks could not batter their way through Troy’s defenses in more than ten years. Even with the collected firepower of antiquity’s Avengers at the gates. In mere days Odysseus transforms the Trojans’ own assumptions into a weapon and persuades his enemy to carry their own defeat home as a trophy.

Read one way, it is a story about the triumph of human ingenuity. Read another, it is a story about what happens when the smartest man in the room decides that every obstacle to his goal is merely a test of his ingenuity and rules are just excuses made by lesser men.

“In the book,” as they say, Homer keeps the Horse at a distance. The Odyssey begins after Troy has fallen and the war is delivered primarily through memories, songs, and the stories of others. When Odysseus asks the bard Demodocus to sing of the Horse, he’s basically requesting his own highlight reel. “Yo DJ, play that shit where I win the war.”

What’s interesting is that Demodocus obliges and Odysseus weeps. He doesn’t shed a single, dignified tear like the stoic warrior of some fantasy novel. Odysseus weeps so violently that Homer compares him to a woman clinging to the body of her murdered husband while her city burns around her and she is dragged away into slavery. But why?

Search your feelings, young Odi; you know it to be true…

Except Homer does not tell us exactly what Odysseus finds there. The grief is explicit. The remorse is inferred. It is Homer who places the conquering hero beside a woman suffering the consequences of conquest, and we are the ones who have to sit with that comparison and decide what it means.

Maybe Odysseus is mourning his comrades. Maybe he is mourning himself. I happen to think there is something darker and much more damning beneath it. Whatever we may think he is crying about, Homer makes his tears look like hers.

That is a hell of an image to put inside a victory song, and Homer lets it hang in the air in much the same way that bricks simply don’t. He doesn’t explain it. He doesn’t give Odysseus a narrated montage of understanding or an opportunity to stare stoically into the middle distance while Hans Zimmer violently assaults a pipe organ. He simply pulls the pin on that bomb and lets the audience wonder whether it’s actually going to explode. Nolan takes that bomb and gives it a whole movie.

Nolan’s opening act doesn’t start with the gods in council and an exposition of petulant deities and divine intervention to set the stage. Nolan starts on the Trojan shoreline where he drags the Horse out of reverie into a humid, suffocating opening movement and turns it into body horror.

We go inside it. We sit with the Greeks in the dark, baking in a wooden oven and half-buried in the wet sand of a sweltering beach. We watch as they perch silently on the rotting corpses of their friends unlucky enough to end up trapped lower than some previous day’s high tide line. We watch as they desperately try to save others doomed to the same fate with improvised snorkels, waves relentlessly flooding the horse as it sinks deeper and deeper into the sand. We watch them gripping their fellow soldiers’ hands as they silently drown. Maybe it’s out of a desire to provide some final bit of comfort, but maybe it’s simply to keep them from thrashing in their death throes and ruining the gambit. Spielberg made us endure the landing at Omaha Beach. Nolan makes us endure the inside of the Horse. In both treatments, the abstract tactical diagrams give way to real human bodies.

You can smell it through the screen.

This is what I meant when I said that Nolan reframes The Odyssey to focus on the ēthos instead of the kléos, and the Horse is just the first of a dozen revisions in as many minutes. Nolan makes no apologies for incorporating elements from various poets throughout the film, including the works of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Especially relevant here is the character Sinon from Virgil’s Aeneid—a reimagining of Homer’s epic from the Trojan perspective as a nationalist Roman origin story centuries later. But unlike Virgil’s Sinon—a conniving, treacherous Greek spy who knows the true nature of the Horse and tricks Rome’s forefathers into tearing down their walls to bring it into the city—Nolan’s Sinon is the virtuous soldier and true believer, used as unsuspecting bait by his commanding officer and kept in the dark regarding the plan and the contents of the Horse.

Believing his compatriots had departed and the Horse was empty, Sinon proclaims to the Trojans that he was left behind to guard the Horse, a gift to Athena to solicit favorable winds. Unlike Virgil’s tale, which relies on both divine intervention by Minerva and the deceit of Sinon to advance the plot, it’s immediately clear that in Nolan’s version this scheme was the work of one man—Odysseus—and that Nolan’s anti-hero is clever enough to have known exactly what the Trojans needed to hear and ruthless enough to sacrifice anyone and everyone to sell it. Odysseus does not need Sinon to be a convincing liar. He needs him to be an honest man. Sinon believes what he says, and that belief is what makes him useful. Even Sinon’s naive integrity becomes part of the weapon. The plan works. Sinon dies, Troy burns and a clever maneuver intended to conclude a war becomes the inciting event that topples a civilization. Game over.

Even Sinon’s naive integrity becomes part of the weapon.

This opener sits in stark contrast to the Homeric version of the story where the gods recount Odysseus’ many virtues, take up his cause, and set about manipulating the required characters to set Odysseus on his journey with Athena as orchestrator and advocate. Nolan’s story is not the grand trials and tribulations of a larger-than-life mortal in defiance of a divine creation. It’s a story of a complicated man. More specifically, it’s the story of a broken man, haunted by the consequences of his actions, tortured by guilt, and stuck between his ambition and his conscience. Odysseus eventually realizes that the Horse was not just a means to an end, but the beginning of the end. It was a violation of xenía—ritualized friendship, hospitality, the Law of Zeus—the social contract which allowed civilization to remain civil. If Agamemnon’s conquest pushed Achaean civilization into the coffin, Odysseus nailed it shut in the shape of a horse. Nolan is not subtle about it.

In Odysseus’ mind, Agamemnon is an imposing, faceless warlord demanding victory by any means necessary. Odysseus cuts down farmers and razes villages to secure supplies while lamenting that they resisted and did not practice xenía, giving over their resources willingly. He chastises Circe for turning his men to pigs only for her to respond that they were always pigs; she just revealed their base nature. He tries to keep eating the lotus. He wants to forget. The souls of his dead soldiers wait on the shores of Erebos to confront him. Nolan makes it painfully clear that Odysseus’ hamartía was that to end the war, he weaponized the trust and decency that built his own civilization. He took advantage of custom and tradition to win. The enemy, on the other hand, still believed that a sacred offering might be sacred. That surrender might mean surrender. That even enemies are bound by honor and propriety. This is Nolan’s most consequential revision. The Horse does not merely end the war; it damages the moral architecture that allows a civilized world to exist after the war is over. Odysseus exploited the very rules that defined his idea of home. Nolan’s Odyssey is less Greek myth and more Ken Burns’ documentary as we follow Odysseus around the Aegean, suffering the consequences of his own military policy first-hand.

There is an old saying that rules constrain the clever and protect the foolish. I’d say that’s generally sound wisdom. Sometimes obedience is the sin. Rules are blunt instruments. Institutions ossify. Traditions can preserve injustice long after anyone remembers why they were created. Antigone is not wrong because Creon upholds the policy of Thebes. Prometheus does not obey simply because Zeus has the power to punish him. There can be virtue and righteousness in disobedience. But that does not mean that every rule is merely a draconic obstacle awaiting disruption. Some institutions are load-bearing. Demolish the wrong wall and the entire structure collapses.

A promise, a guest-right, a truce, a court, an election, a contract, a scientific norm, a shared understanding of what evidence counts—none of these things is physically real in the way that a spear or a city wall is real. They are constructs that exist because enough people agree to behave as though they exist, including when honoring them is inconvenient. Especially when honoring them is inconvenient. The clever can usually find an exception to the rule and the powerful can frequently survive making one. The demagogue learns to confuse those facts with righteousness.

One more war crime to end the war…

One more suspension of civil liberties to end the emergency…

One more regulatory exemption so the brilliant founder can change the world…

One more term for dear leader…

One more bailout without accountability…

One more immiseration to reach the singularity…

Just this once, for the greater good…

Don’t you want to win?

We are rather fond of Odysseus at the moment, it would seem. We call him founder, patriot, visionary, outlier. He’s an impact player with atomic habits—a bona fide doer. He practices no-rules rules and radical candor while measuring what matters to six sigma. He’s agile. He moves fast and breaks shit. He’s a Disruptor.

We celebrate the person who sees the loophole, routes around the bureaucracy, ignores the conventional wisdom, and gets shit done while everyone else is still scheduling a steering committee. Disruption is just Mētis with a Series A in her pocket and her eye on an S-1 filing. And sometimes that person is exactly what the moment requires. The difference between medicine and poison, however, is in the dose.

I have made an entire career out of adaptation. I am a technical program manager working in machine learning and artificial intelligence at a Fortune 50 company without a computer science degree. Nobody handed me the approved map from Ilios to Ithaca. I learned the terrain, found side doors, accumulated the necessary skills, and became useful enough that the absence of formal credentials ceased to settle the question. I did not get here by accident. I also did not get here through the prescribed channels, and I too did suffer, on occasion, the slings and arrows of my own hubris upon the wine-dark sea.

So believe me when I say that I am not making an argument against cunning. I am temperamentally, professionally, and perhaps pathologically inclined to find another way through. If the game is stupid, I will complain about the game—and then I will learn the rules well enough to win it.

The uncomfortable question is “What happens next?” And this is the focus of Nolan’s camera. Not gods, not glory, not resilience, but ethics. The principled opportunist is clever. The unprincipled opportunist is a jackal. Oftentimes, in the moment, it’s hard to see the difference.

Adaptation requires constraints. Agile, properly understood, does not mean changing the destination every time the road becomes difficult. It means holding the purpose firmly enough that the plan survives contact with the enemy. Hypotheses are proven wrong, strategies are rebuilt, the tactics are re-litigated, but the values must remain stable. I expect a plan to change when it encounters reality. I also expect to remain responsible for what I do while changing it. Discovering that a promise is inconvenient does not make it a failed hypothesis. If the only fixed value is victory, everything else becomes negotiable: promises, obligations, friends, strangers, the gods, the dead, and the definition of ‘home’ itself. The cleverness that once served victory begins consuming the only reasons that victory ever mattered. This is the Odyssey that Nolan takes us on, and if it feels uncomfortably close to home, good. That’s the point.

Discovering that a promise is inconvenient does not make it a failed hypothesis.

Nolan may borrow from Virgil, but his Odysseus is not some cackling Disney villain. No one is the villain in their own story. He does not destroy Troy because he enjoys suffering. Nolan’s Odysseus has spent ten years in a war he did not begin and that he would have rather avoided, surrounded by men who just want to go home. The Horse ends a war where brute force, endless siege, and hopeless endurance have all failed. That matters. It probably saves thousands of Greek lives. That matters too.

Refusing to employ the trick would have carried a cost. Employing the trick carried a cost to Sinon and others before Troy ever paid a price. Ambiguity does not eliminate responsibility; it intensifies it. The absence of a spotless option does not mean every available option becomes morally equivalent. It means every option carries a real consequence that must be carefully weighed. There is no clean solution to vindicate the action. The Horse may have saved Greek lives compared with another year of siege. But is another year of siege the only imaginable solution?

Could they have negotiated? Could they have simply left? Could they have endured the humiliation of failing? Odysseus’ statements would have us believe that Agamemnon would never have allowed it, but his memories betray his uncertainty and guilt. If every outcome except victory is excluded in advance, “we had no other choice” begins to sound suspiciously convenient.

Moral conflict is not resolved by simply pretending that consequences only exist on one side of the ledger. The test of mētis, then, cannot simply be whether the tactic succeeds. We must inspect the goal, the cost, and what survives in its wake. Would we accept the method as legitimate if our enemy or our neighbor or our boss or our government used it against us? Most importantly, when the event has passed and the dust has settled, will what’s left still contain the things we claim to defend? Will we?

Odysseus does not fail the test because he is unintelligent. He fails because he has become so skilled at escaping the rules of society that he can no longer distinguish an unjust constraint from a necessary one. Nolan shows this over and over. It’s visible in Athena’s constant sorrow. It’s in Telemachus’ warning to treat the stranger as you would treat the gods. It’s shown through the towering facelessness of Agamemnon that haunts Odysseus’ memories, his increasingly disorganized retreats from each fresh hell and his constant laments and indignant justifications. The king of Ithaca who would sooner starve than ambush his prey stands silent on the shoulders of his rotting troops, waiting to murder an entire city asleep in their beds.

Nolan, like Homer before him, recognizes that intelligence and deceit share a border. Homer’s Odysseus lies beautifully, manipulates constantly, and repeatedly converts other people’s trust into the currency of his own survival. Nolan does not invent darkness in a hero who was previously made of light. He simply elects to change where the light falls.

Nolan’s Horse does not merely enter Troy. It enters Odysseus, and he spends the next ten years carrying it home.
