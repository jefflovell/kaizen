The Fuzzy Logic of Nolan’s Odyssey

Part 4 · 23 min read

Fate vs. Free Will and the Problem of Accountability

What happens when the gods stop explaining us to ourselves?

Poseidon is angry. The gods are testing me. The prophecy must be fulfilled. Terrible things have happened, certainly, but at least the universe has provided an explanation. There is somebody upstairs. There is a reason. Maybe there is even a complaint procedure, provided you have a sufficiently impressive bull and can make it to the local planning office in Alpha Centauri to file your petition.

There is a peculiar comfort in knowing whose fault it is, even when the answer is that a temperamental sea god has decided to make your commute home take ten years. People, on the other hand, as anyone in a PTA or HOA can tell you, are considerably less accommodating.

People remember things differently. They disagree about what is or isn't necessary. They have different concerns and they insist on bringing up the part of your glorious victory where their children died over and over and over. They're insufferable! No matter how many times you explain the strategic value of labor efficiency to the company's quarterly earnings, the proletariat will manage to remain stubbornly unimpressed by your PowerPoints at the All Hands meeting and keep writing nasty things about you on Blind. At its core, I believe Nolan's framing of Odysseus to be the story of a man who has spent his life finding ways around obstacles finally encountering something he cannot outmaneuver: the possibility that his account of himself is wrong and, more importantly, how he handles it. The Homeric version gives us an excellent explanation for everything that has happened to Odysseus. Nolan's hero, meanwhile, is beginning to suspect that an explanation is not the same thing as an excuse.

So as I was saying, no. Homer did not write a deterministic puppet show and wait three thousand years for Christopher Nolan to discover free will. Neither are the Greek gods some coherent, comforting Supreme Court of the universe. They disagree. They play favorites. They burn down entire cities, although to be fair, usually they take more than a couple episodes in a truncated final season to set it up. Point being, sometimes the person with the most convincing moral argument is standing directly beneath the lightning bolt. That's just how the cookie crumbles.

One of the more cited examples is Oedipus. By all accounts, Oedipus was a good king; great even. Maybe a little harsh, certainly haughty, but hey, heavy is the head. He was fair. He was just. He didn't do anything particularly egregious to deserve his fate other than trying like hell to avoid it. It was the belief in his doom and his attempts to avoid his fate which ended up leading him right into it. There's that mētis again! Some things are simply out of our control. What makes it an interesting story is watching how he reacts to those events. Oedipus was warned repeatedly that this was his fault and advised to stop his dogged pursuit. He refused to believe it and kept doubling down until he was all in. He couldn't let go of his certainty and became the victim of his own judgement. That's his hamartía, not their judgement. He's a literary train wreck that the whole audience sees coming. He should have brushed up on his Sophocles. Divine involvement and human responsibility were never mutually exclusive in The Odyssey nor were they in Classical Greece. The gods are arguing with each other about Odysseus' fate long before Odysseus ever gets an opportunity to whine about taking an arrow to the knee.

This is a critical and often overlooked point in the Greek composition. The gods are not necessarily fair or just, or even all that interested. Oftentimes they are, and that's a typical source of catharsis in Greek stories but they are also frequently capricious, selfish, or even downright feckless. What the gods do provide is an ontological framework of the human world. They are a more-or-less orderly and intelligible relationship between the human world and the things that lie beyond it, whatever they may be. There are powers to petition, obligations to honor, and offenses to explain—just like there are in the human world. A terrifying and overwhelming universe can still be a universe with a head of household and an address.

The difference between Homer and Nolan is that Nolan takes away our privileged access to that address. We do not sit through the divine deliberations and then watch those same decisions trickle down like Reaganomics with full color commentary from a chorus of talking heads. There are substantially fewer divine warnings in the film than in the text. Athena remains, but her watchfulness is easier to read as Odysseus' confrontation with his own conscience than as a reliable briefing on the gods’ operating model. I think it's a welcome change and appropriate for a modern audience. The absence of explicitly divine meddling in the film does not make the whirlpool less dangerous or the cyclops less peckish, but it does make the people in the boat harder to ignore and I firmly believe that was Nolan's intent.

Was Odysseus warned beforehand? Was the crew? Who decided to sail? Who gets to give the orders? And who gets to drown or get eaten by monsters for following them? How much of what we are calling divine intervention is just weather? And how much of the crew's fate is just the captain’s unwillingness to admit that he made a bad decision? I am not proposing to resolve the metaphysics of free will between a Cyclops and its snack break but these same circumstances also expose a much more practical question: how do we distinguish a constraint from an alibi?

02

"What happened to me?" vs "What have I done?"

Odysseus has suffered. That is not in dispute. He is as often a victim of others as he is the villain. The trouble begins when suffering becomes the whole defense.

The language of moral injury is useful here. It describes the lasting distress that can follow participating in, witnessing, or failing to prevent something that violates a person’s deeply held values; betrayal by trusted authorities can cause moral injury as well whether it's parents, bosses, clergy, or commanding officers. "I was just following orders" has never been a particularly winning strategy whether at Nuremberg or in front of the mirror. Moral injury overlaps with PTSD, but they are not interchangeable, and moral injury is not itself a psychiatric diagnosis. The boundaries are fuzzy and the definitions are nuanced but at the risk of oversimplifying, the difference between the two is roughly that PTSD emphasizes threats to the person, while moral injury emphasizes threats to identity. "The world is dangerous and I am unsafe" versus "I have been betrayed by someone I trusted" or "I have failed to do what is right". Both can contain feelings of guilt and remorse but, most crucially, those feelings of guilt do not require or establish that someone was culpable for the cause of those feelings. In other words, you can feel guilty for things that aren't your fault. VA National Center for PTSD.

That last distinction matters deeply and cannot be overstated. However, while feeling guilty does not prove that you did something wrong, neither does feeling guilty about something you did wrong settle the debt. Odysseus’ suffering deserves our compassion; it does not oblige the people he harmed to forgive him, and it certainly does not make their suffering a supporting character in the story of his recovery. A soldier can carry an impossible burden for something he could not have prevented. A commander can sleep beautifully after something he absolutely could. Conscience is not an infallible court any more than the gods are. But as a way of reading Nolan’s Odysseus, moral injury opens into a tragically beautiful question that resilience and adaptability alone cannot answer. Not merely, How do I survive what happened to me? but also How do I live with what I did?

Feeling guilty does not prove that you did something wrong, but neither does feeling guilty about something you did wrong settle the debt.

The second question does not cancel or even diminish the first. Odysseus can have been used by Agamemnon and also have used Sinon. He can love his men while spending their lives. He can desperately want to go home and have laid waste to someone else's. We humans are full of contradictions and internal conficts. That is the messy truth of people. That is the film.

That is also why I find the lotus substantially more disturbing than the prospect of another monster. A monster gives Odysseus something external to fight. We're right back to lógos and kléos: rationality and glory. Odysseus' desire to forget turns that focus inward to ēthos. Not "what happened to me?" but ,"what have I done?" It's a very powerful shift. Forgetting offers him a way to remain the hero of a story that he no longer has to examine because the dead—inconvenient bastards that they are—keep turning up with their own version and won't leave him the hell alone about his (lack of) honor and duty.

We have become exceedingly comfortable in recent times saying that people are the products of their circumstances. They absolutely are but that's also not a hot take. The nature vs nurture debate has been around for a good, long while. What I have trouble with is the moment that observation becomes a complete defense, as though explaining how a person arrived at a decision settles whether the resultant actions were justified. I am willing to believe that Odysseus was exhausted, frightened, under orders, and desperate to get his men home. I am less willing to accept that those circumstances made every alternative disappear, particularly when he is the one telling me which alternatives were available. Hellooooo? Conflict of interest, anyone? It's giving unreliable narrator... Big if true.

If we have any meaningful degree of choice, then an honest accounting has to include what we could have done differently, what that would have cost, and who paid for the choices we actually made. That buck has to stop somewhere and we killed the gods and burned down the house three states ago, we're on the lam. Context can widen our compassion but it can also become an exquisitely furnished place to hide from accountability. If my pain or need justifies what I do to you, whose pain gets the final word? Nolan’s film does not become interesting because it supplies a satisfying answer. It becomes interesting because Odysseus’ usual answers stop satisfying him.

This existential tension is actually a surprisingly common theme in modern dramas, and for good reason. Art imitates life, and our lives have become increasingly complex and increasingly abstract over exponentially shorter periods of time. It took us two million years to get from stone tools to fire. Gunpowder and the printing press were within a few short centuries of each other and a few more later we're now living in glass skyscrapers and zipping around in dreamliners, while asking AI to fact-check the TikTok video we got beamed from outer space to the supercomputer in our pocket. Life moves pretty fast these days if you don't stop and look around once in awhile.

Just as the gods reflected the virtues and morals of the ancients, the "character arc" does so for those of us living in this modern age. How many times have you heard the criticism about flat characters ruining a production? They're one-dimensional! They aren't believable! Nobody would react like that! The writing sucks! We can set characters zipping around the galaxy blasting death rays out of their eyeballs and yet for some reason, what audiences tend to get stuck on is how realistically the actors interact with other characters and how they react to their circumstances. Go figure. I fully admit that I do it too. We seem to have a distinctly different set of conditions for suspending disbelief from previous societies. I suspect that it has something to do with the fact that we have delved so deeply into the physical weirdness of our reality that we just have a fundamentally different definition of plausibility from most of our ancestors both recent and ancient. We're somehow both more tightly coupled to, and separated from our physical world than ever before.

Regardless, it strikes me as natural that we tend to criticize behavior and motivation while hand-waving away technology and magic when we inhabit a world which is so recently and in so many ways unimaginably different from the rest of recorded history. I don't think that the difference is that previous audiences lacked an interest in the inner world, but that so much of our drama now asks that inner world to explain what our explanations of the physical world no longer reliably explain for us. Prior to the 20th century, drama—and more specifically, Western drama—relied heavily on a notion of "grand narratives"—overarching, external systems of meaning like religion, science, or political progress that reflected a moral and well-ordered universe. It's tried-and-true. You'd sit down and strap in. The curtain ascends. There's a bit of explanation and introduction so you can latch the pattern and get invested... and weeeeeeeeeeee!Here comes the conflict! Which, of course, you know is coming; a few twists and turns for effect, maybe a loop or two, and finally there's a resolution and you walk away satisfied. That tension and release generates catharsis. Along the way, there's usually a lesson in there somewhere of how all this suffering belongs to an intelligible order, even if nobody involved is going to be remotely OK. You get to experience the stressful event—and then release the stress at the end. It's satisfying. And historically, it's a proven winner. It's why everyone else always coos over your children and tells you how sweet and well behaved they are. They get the good stuff and then they get to go home. The unprecedented industrialization of the 19th century ushered in an age of existentialism to which the 20th century nonchalantly responded, "Hold my beer".

As industrialization spread, cities expanded, and populations boomed, the modern individual in Western culture suddenly began to feel increasingly isolated and powerless despite—or maybe because of—a break-neck pace of technological progress, wealth, and connectivity. The rapidly shrinking world somehow managed to simultaneously become bigger and increasingly threatening. Wagons and horses gave way to planes, trains, and automobiles; and we could suddenly get around the world in eighty days like Verne always imagined. Just as the gods gave the ancients a map by which to explain their terrifyingly unknowable world, psychology provides us a map for our terrifyingly rationalized one. The market made me do it

Art frequently leads the charge, and the most illustrious of artists frequently die impoverished martyrs of their own times, only posthumously canonized by some future vicar as disciples of The Truth whether they're insufferably stubborn business leaders or politicans who don't accept the status quo or illiterate, black boys playing devil music for Mississippi Delta sharecroppers. Fighting the power is more easily said than done. Who would fardels bear to grunt and sweat under a weary life, but that dread of something after death? Who indeed? We have not yet outgrown this desire for a force that justifies our choices while relieving us of responsibility for them. Nay, say I. If anything, it has merely intensified. For who would bear the whips and scorns of time? The oppressor's wrong, the proud man's contumely, the panges of despised love, the law's delay, the insolence of office and the spurns that patient merit of the unworthy takes—when he, himself, might his quietus make with a bare bodkin? And thus the native hue of resolution is sicklied over with the pale cast of thought and enterprises of great pith and moment; with this regard their currents turn awry 😔. It's not a new story. We've just given it new names.

The Market. The Algorithm. Progress. Tradition. Precedent. National Security.

Do you hear that, Mr. Anderson? That is the sound of inevitability. Don't fight it. It's just the way it is. Sometimes these labels identify real constraints. More often they just obfuscate the people who set the constraint and stand to benefit from everyone else accepting it.

I work in machine learning, artificial intelligence, and personalization. I am not looking at Silicon Valley from some distant monastery, wondering whether electricity was a mistake. I find this work fascinating. I also know that an optimization system requires something to optimize. A metric has to be selected. A tradeoff has to be accepted. Someone has to decide which harms appear on the dashboard and which get made into somebody else’s problem and the projects we write post-mortems about aren't typically the pleasant ones. “The algorithm did it” is an explanation that should begin an investigation, not end one.

Cory Doctorow’s concept of enshittification is useful here because it identifies a mechanism: platforms can exploit locked-in users and businesses when competition, regulation, labor, and interoperability cease to constrain them effectively. The decay has beneficiaries and enabling conditions. It is not simply the natural aging process of a software product. Cory Doctorow, “Who broke the internet?”. Call it late-stage capitalism if you like, but now we're right back to Thunder Daddy. What I want to know is what the phrase is accusing somebody of doing. As a decent human being, I am royally pissed, because... Which obligation was weakened? Who changed the incentive? Who made leaving harder? If we stop at the label, capitalism becomes yet another moody deity whose displeasure must simply be endured.

Regulatory capture makes this evasion particularly elegant: shape the rules, then appeal to the rules as evidence that your hands are tied. The devil's greatest trick was convincing the world that he doesn't exist. Social media gives the rest of us a smaller version of the same temptation. I can deplore the outrage machine while feeding it, then describe my participation as something the algorithm baited me into doing. The asymmetry of power is real and so is the conveniently packaged deflection of personal responsibility in that sentence. AI can be both a promised land and an apocalypse. The future will be so abundant that present objections look petty; or, the future will be so dangerous that extraordinary concentrations of power become necessary to manage it. Por que no los dos? Just don't open the box, duh. Those are not obviously not equivalent forecasts, and serious safety research is not obviously doomerism. So why are so many of us not immediately suspicious when asked to surrender judgment to the people standing outside the big-ass wooden horse? These are, after all, the same people who spent the last decade trying to sack the city. This has the feeling of ornery, bearded forces at work. Best not to question it. Is the investment boom a bubble? How capable will the systems become? Important questions. Neither answers whether a particular deployment, labor decision, or transfer of power is justified now. “This is inevitable” is doing a lot of work in an argument about things that we mortals are actively building.

I am not suggesting that Nolan hid a position paper on any of these subjects inside the Cyclops’ cave. Nor should we confuse responsibility with the fantasy that sufficient virtue gives us control over everything. Sometimes the storm really is a storm. The gods are fickle, but so is the weather. Accountability means being specific about what people do and don't do—not simply finding someone to punish because uncertainty is intolerable. But if Nolan's brooding, prosaic Odysseus has a song to sing, it is to resist the temptation to describe the world we are helping make as a world in which we can only obey. Personally, I'd prefer not to be turned into a paperclip.

05

We are the people from the sea

To bring it full circle back to the opening of the film, war makes our group tendency of deferential obeisance to the inevitable especially consequential. War is messy, justifications are easy to come by, long term consequences are a problem for tomorrow and life gets very cheap, very quickly. Human adaptation is a double edged sword.

The post-9/11 disputes over “enemy combatants” were not merely arguments about vocabulary. They concerned detention, legal protections, and the power to decide whose status placed them outside the ordinary rules. Iraq and Afghanistan did not invent moral ambiguity nor war the wars of the twentieth century a reliably tidy arrangement of good guys and bad guys, however efficiently the victory montages may sort the uniforms today. But a war against a tactic or an ideology exposes a particularly topical problem: who can surrender on its behalf? What event establishes "Mission Accomplished"?

The ICRC challenged the notion that those classifications could leave people in a legal vacuum. ICRC: challenges from the “War on Terror”. The rhetoric of necessity should feel familiar. We are defending civilization. The enemy does not honor our rules. We cannot afford to be sentimental. We will restore normality once we have secured the conditions that make normality possible. Nothing is forbidden. Everything is permitted. And in the meantime, people have to carry out the orders. Everyone else has to live with the consequences. Odysseus can recall his story as a series of dangers encountered on the way home. The people on those shores might tell it as the arrival of armed strangers who wanted their food and would not accept no. The captain’s account and the village’s account describe the same landing but they do not describe the same experience.

This is why the Sea Peoples revelation as portrayed in the film lands for me. The civilization-threatening force is no longer safely somewhere beyond the horizon. The men we have followed, the men whose hardships we understand, can themselves become that force. Nolan invents this identification as part of his fictional treatment of the Bronze Age’s end; it is not an archaeological solution to the historical Sea Peoples question. The film’s ending. But it makes just as much sense as midichorians and green superheroes do as allegory for the duality of man. Destruction follows from people pursuing goals they consider necessary while treating the conditions of everybody else’s survival as negotiable. Self-destruction follows from absolving oneself of responsibility for one's own actions. However, “We are the Sea Peoples” does not mean that everyone is equally guilty. The person setting the course does not have the same freedom as the person chained to the oar. If collective responsibility makes those differences disappear, it becomes just another way for the powerful to spread the bill around and shirk the yoke of accountability. But neither can we quietly mean that it's purely the fault of those assholes over there. Not the billionaires, the politicians, the generals, or the anonymous bootlickers in somebody else’s comment section—while I remain a morally intact spectator enjoying the show. I have spent these essays explaining why I recognize myself in Odysseus. It would be awfully convenient to stop recognizing him precisely when he becomes difficult to defend.

I want to believe I am adaptable. I want to believe I am useful. I want to believe that when I find a way around an obstacle, I know the difference between a pointless restriction and somebody else’s protection. Those are pleasant things to believe about myself, but evidence of virtue they are not. The mark of virtue is not in what I had to suffer on the way home. It is in the consequences to other people because of my actions while I was trying to get there—and whether I can bear to hear the account without attempting to immediately justify it.

We are the Sea Peoples. The gods did not build this Horse. I did. And so did you.
