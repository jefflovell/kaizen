The Fuzzy Logic of Nolan’s Odyssey

Part 4 · 23 min read

Fate vs. Free Will and the Problem of Accountability

What happens when the gods stop explaining us to ourselves?

Part 1 Prologue Part 2 Taxidermy Part 3 The Horse Part 4 Accountability

Poseidon is angry. The gods are testing me. The prophecy must be fulfilled. Terrible things have happened, certainly, but at least the universe has provided an explanation. There is somebody upstairs. There is a reason. Maybe there is even a complaint procedure, provided you have a sufficiently impressive bull and can make it to the local planning office in Alpha Centauri to file your petition. There is a peculiar comfort in knowing whose fault it is, even when the answer is that a temperamental sea god has decided to make your commute home take ten years.

People, on the other hand, as anyone in a PTA or HOA can tell you, are considerably less accommodating.

People remember things differently. They disagree about what is or isn't necessary. They have different concerns and they insist on bringing up the part of your glorious victory where their children died over and over and over. They're insufferable. You can explain the strategic value of labor efficiency to the company's quarterly earnings until you are blue in the face, but the proletariat will somehow remain stubbornly unimpressed by your PowerPoint at the All Hands meeting. Don't they understand that we had no choice? Won't somebody think of the profit margins?! The Company has a fiduciary responsibility to our shareholders! (They don't, by the way, and for the officers that do, there is no freestanding duty to maximize immediate profits so please stop regurgitating this nonsense on Reddit.)

In the previous installment, I argued that Nolan’s Horse gets inside Odysseus and stays there. This is the installment where that becomes more than a particularly unpleasant metaphor so get ready for gratuitous hyperlinking and MLA-style documentation. I dug out all my college texts and I'm about to drop some really expensive liberal arts knowledge for free, you're welcome. So like I was saying about the fairytale about the pony and the boat and the sad man...

At its core, I believe Nolan's framing of Odysseus to be about a man who has spent his life finding ways around obstacles finally encountering something he cannot outmaneuver: the possibility that his account of himself is wrong, and how he handles it. The Homeric version gives us an excellent explanation for everything that has happened to Odysseus. Nolan's hero is beginning to suspect that an explanation is not the same thing as an excuse.

01

Before I pull out the cocaine and give Odysseus the ole Austrian Analysis, a brief explanation and disclosure is owed. Homer did not write a deterministic puppet show and wait three thousand years for Christopher Nolan to discover free will. Even he recognized that there is a rather large, bearded complication to free will sitting on Olympus and he provided a reasonable if convenient explanation as to why Zeus didn't just step in and fix everything.

Early in Book I, Zeus complains that mortals blame the gods for troubles they bring upon themselves. His example is Aegisthus, who was explicitly warned about the consequences of taking Agamemnon’s wife and murdering her husband. I mean explicitly. Hermes literally takes him aside and says hey bud...do not seduce Clytemnestra. Do not kill Agamemnon. If you do, Orestes is going to absolutely lose his shit. He did it anyway. Side note: fun fact, this cautionary tale sets the stakes for Odysseus' return. It is paralleled turn-for-turn in the story of Penelope and the Suitors. Like Clytemnestra, Penelope is left alone while her husband is away at the Trojan War. Like Aegisthus, the Suitors swoop in to seduce the queen and steal the kingdom. Like Hermes, Athena warns that this behavior will lead to catastrophe. Like Orestes, Telemachus takes revenge. The details are different, but the major beats are all there. This, boys and girls, is what we call foreshadowing. Write that down. No, it's F-O-R-E...Odyssey, Book I, 32–43.

So as I was saying, no. Homer did not write a deterministic puppet show and wait three thousand years for Christopher Nolan to discover free will. Neither are the Greek gods some coherent, comforting Supreme Court of the universe. They disagree. They play favorites. Sometimes the person with the most convincing moral argument is standing directly beneath the lightning bolt. Oedipus was a good king. He did everything he could to avoid his fate which led him right into it. Some things are out of our control. What's interesting is how we react to those events. Oedipus was warned repeatedly that this was his fault and advised to stop his dogged pursuit. He refused to believe it and kept doubling down until he was all in. He couldn't let go of his certainty and became the victim of his own judgement. That's his hamartía, not their judgement. He should have brushed up on his Sophocles. Divine involvement and human responsibility were never mutually exclusive in this poem nor were they in Classical Greece. The gods are arguing with each other about Odysseus' fate long before Odysseus even gets an opportunity to take an arrow to the knee.

This is a critical and often overlooked point. The gods are not necessarily fair or just, or even all that interested. Oftentimes they are, and that's a typical source of catharsis in Greek stories but they are also frequently capricious, selfish, or even downright feckless. What the gods do provide is an ontological framework of the human world. They are a more-or-less orderly and intelligible relationship between the human world and the things that lie beyond it, whatever they may be. There are powers to petition, obligations to honor, offenses to explain, just like there are in the human world. A terrifying and overwhelming universe can still be a universe with a head of household and an address.

Nolan takes away our privileged access to that address. We do not sit through the divine deliberations and then watch their decisions trickle down like Reaganomics. There are substantially fewer divine warnings in the film than in the text. Athena remains, but her watchfulness is easier to read as a confrontation with conscience than as a reliable briefing on the gods’ operational plan. The absence of explicitly divine meddling in the film does not make the whirlpool less dangerous or the Cyclops less peckish but it does make the people in the boat harder to ignore and I firmly believe that was Nolan's intent.

Was Odysseus warned beforehand? Was the crew? Who decided to sail? Who gets to give the orders? And who gets to drown or get eaten by monsters for following them? How much of what we are calling divine intervention is just weather? And how much of the crew's fate is just the captain’s unwillingness to admit that he made a bad decision?

I am not proposing to resolve the metaphysics of free will between a Cyclops and its snack break. The question here is more practical: how can we distinguish a constraint from an alibi?

02

"What happened to me?" vs "What have I done?"

Odysseus has suffered. That is not in dispute. He is as often a victim of others as he is the villain. The trouble begins when suffering becomes the whole defense.

The language of moral injury is useful here. It describes the lasting distress that can follow participating in, witnessing, or failing to prevent something that violates a person’s deeply held values; betrayal by trusted authorities can cause moral injury as well whether it's parents, bosses, clergy, or commanding officers. "I was just following orders" has never been a particularly winning strategy whether at Nuremberg or in front of the mirror. Moral injury overlaps with PTSD, but they are not interchangeable, and moral injury is not itself a psychiatric diagnosis. The boundary is fuzzy but at the risk of oversimplifying, the difference between the two is roughly that PTSD emphasizes threats to the person, while moral injury emphasizes threats to identity. "The world is dangerous and I am unsafe" versus "I have been betrayed by someone I trusted" or "I have failed to do what is right". Both can contain feelings of guilt and remorse but, most crucially, those feelings of guilt do not require or establish that someone was culpable for the cause of those feelings. In other words, you can feel guilty for things that aren't your fault. VA National Center for PTSD.

That last distinction matters deeply and cannot be overstated. Feeling guilty does not prove that you did something wrong, but neither does feeling guilty about something you did wrong settle the debt. Odysseus’ suffering deserves our compassion; it does not oblige the people he harmed to forgive him, and it certainly does not make their suffering a supporting character in the story of his recovery. A soldier can carry an impossible burden for something he could not have prevented. A commander can sleep beautifully after something he absolutely could. Conscience is not an infallible court any more than the gods are. But as a way of reading Nolan’s Odysseus, moral injury opens into a tragically beautiful question that resilience and adaptability alone cannot answer. Not merely, How do I survive what happened to me? but also How do I live with what I did?

Feeling guilty does not prove that you did something wrong, but neither does feeling guilty about something you did wrong settle the debt.

The second question does not cancel the first. Odysseus can have been used by Agamemnon and also have used Sinon. He can love his men while spending their lives. He can desperately want to go home and have laid waste to someone else's. That is the messy truth of people. That is the film.

That is also why I find the lotus more disturbing than the prospect of another monster. A monster gives Odysseus something external to fight. We're right back to lógos and kléos: rationality and glory. The desire to forget turns the focus inward to ēthos. Not what happened to me but what I have done. It's a very powerful shift. Forgetting offers him a way to remain the hero of a story that he no longer has to examine because the dead—inconvenient bastards that they are—keep turning up with their own version and won't leave him the hell alone about his (lack of) honor and duty.

We have become very comfortable in recent times saying that people are products of their circumstances. They absolutely are. What I have trouble with is the moment that observation becomes a complete defense, as though explaining how a person arrived at a decision settles whether the decision was justified. I am willing to believe that Odysseus was exhausted, frightened, under orders, and desperate to get his men home. I am less willing to accept that those circumstances made every alternative disappear, particularly when he is the one telling me which alternatives were available. If we have any meaningful degree of choice, then an honest account has to include what we could have done differently, what that would have cost, and who paid for the choice we actually made. Context can widen our compassion; it can also become an exquisitely furnished place to hide. If my pain or need justifies what I do to you, whose pain gets the final word? Nolan’s film does not become interesting because it supplies a satisfying answer. It becomes interesting because Odysseus’ usual answers stop satisfying him.

03

Waiting for Godot to fix it

This tension is familiar territory to anyone who has spent an irresponsible amount of money studying theater. I like to think that playing with this tension is a big part of why I'm good at joining technical concerns with business requirements. I can hold conflicting views without a lot of dissonance. It can also make me a bit of a nightmare to argue with because I have no problem playing devil's advocate. This tension is the lifeblood of drama. Beckett leaves people waiting for an arrival around which they have organized their lives. Ionesco’s Rhinoceros lets the grotesque become socially normal. Stoppard places Rosencrantz and Guildenstern inside a story whose machinery they can neither adequately understand nor escape. These are very different dramatic worlds, but they share a discomfort with each other and with Nolan's Odyssey: having an explanation for your situation does not necessarily tell you how to inhabit it. Waiting for Godot, Rhinoceros, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead.

This existential tension is actually a surprisingly common theme in modern dramas, and for good reason. Art imitates life, and our lives have become increasingly complex and increasingly abstract in a very short period of time. It took us two million years to get from stone tools to fire. It took us another million to get to agriculture. Five thousand years from there to wheels and boats and only a thousand to writing. Gunpowder and the printing press were within a few hundred years of each other and less than five hundred years later we're now living in glass skyscrapers and zipping around in dreamliners while asking AI to fact-check the TikTok video we got beamed from outer space to the supercomputer in our pocket—merely a century after the first Model T rolled off the assembly line. And now, if you will, please indulge me and the skull of my favorite clown in a brief, if pedantic, soliloquy on the matter. I think you'll enjoy it.

Just as the gods reflected the virtues and morals of the ancients, the "character arc" does so for those of us living in this modern age. How many times have you heard the criticism about flat characters ruining a production? They're one-dimensional. They aren't believable. People wouldn't say that. They're trying too hard. We can set characters zipping around the galaxy blasting death rays out of their eyeballs and what audiences get stuck on is how the actor interacts with other characters and how they react to their circumstances. This is a distinctly different set of conditions for suspending disbelief from previous societies. The difference is not that previous audiences lacked an interest in the inner life, but that so much of our drama now asks that inner life to explain what the larger world no longer reliably explains for us.

Prior to the 20th century, drama—and more specifically, Western drama—relied heavily on "grand narratives"—overarching, external systems of meaning like religion, science, or political progress that reflected a moral, well-ordered universe. It's rampant. You sit down. There's some explanation, then there's a conflict, then there's a resolution. That tension and release generates catharsis. Along the way, there's usually a lesson in there somewhere of how all this suffering belongs to an intelligible order, even when nobody involved is going to be remotely OK. You get to experience the stressful event, and then release the stress at the end. It's satisfying, and it's why everyone else always coos over your children and tells you how sweet and well behaved they are. They get the good stuff and then they get to go home. A full-blown tantrum or three is endurable if it has an explicit expiration date. You, on the other hand, have to live it. That emotional transference relies on the suspension of disbelief. You have to get emotionally invested. If something about it knocks you out, like my wife nitpicking the lack of visible propulsion in sci-fi...well, you're probably gonna hate it. That's just how it goes with art. Once you release it into the world, you lose control over it. (This is also why "broad appeal" popular art tends to feel like watered-down slop to a lot of people but that's a different kettle of fish).

All of this is not to say that those stories don't exist anymore. They absolutely do. It's comforting to be led by the nose and just enjoy the spectacle. Just look at the MCU. We still have rom-coms and Hallmark movies. In plenty of movies the good guys always win and the bad guys get their comeuppance. Harry meets Sally, Maverick switches to guns, we avoid disaster through human ingenuity, and everyone lives happily ever after. Goonies never say die except for when it comes to passing the Bechdel test. Neither am I denying that previous societies employed theater as a means of social commentary or criticism of their respective worlds. But there's a distinctively modern vein of more "artistic" art that stands in stark contrast to the last few thousand years of Grand Narratives. Industrialization during the 19th century ushered in the age of existentialism and the 20th century said, "Hold my beer".

In the wake of the industrial revolution and as populations boomed and industrialization spread, the modern individual in Western culture suddenly began to feel increasingly isolated and powerless despite unprecedented technological progress, wealth, and connectivity. The world is shrinking while somehow seeming to get bigger and more threatening. There are planes, trains, and automobiles and we can get around the world in eighty days like Verne always imagined. But people begin to increasingly feel like a cog in the machine. One faceless human adrift in a sea of faceless humans. This is a period of "urban alienation". The growing pains of modernization from which we still suffer today. Adding to the burden of our forebears, the first half of the 20th century was filled with a rather nightmarish number of traumatic events, not just in the US but globally. The Dust Bowl. The Great Depression. Two World Wars, the Spanish Flu, genocide in Armenia, and famine in Russia. The collapse of empires and political revolutions and, of course, the new and existential threat of nuclear annihilation. Inherited accounts of moral order, joined by newer promises of scientific and political progress, begin to buckle under the things this supposedly advancing civilization is doing to itself. People begin to wonder if there will be Darmok and Jalad at Tenagra, because all they can see is Darmok on the ocean.

Modernity has been...a lot. In hindsight, it's no wonder that there was a major shift in how we organize and relate to our world. We may very well be discussing a similar shift fifty or a hundred years from now. It's looking pretty crazy out there right now.

Kierkegaard was the canary in the coal mine. "What I really need to do is get clear about what I must do, not what I must know."

Nietzsche soon after proclaims that "God is dead".

Sartre retorts that "Hell is other people", and Camus laughs at it.

Tolstoy gets depressed and starts rumbling about how the dragons are hanging out in the bottoms of wells now or whatever and Freud goes on a cocaine-fueled rant to explain why it is all so damned funny and obviously because we want to fuck our parents.

Beckett. O'Neill. Brecht. Pirandello. Kafka. Woolf. Joyce. Dostoevsky. Kirchner. Dalí. Munch. The list goes on, and on, and on. The audience of modern art is an audience looking inward to answer the questions that those looking outward claim no longer matter. We are complicated men and women. We are large. We contain multitudes. And that, my friends, is why I think this "authenticity" argument is pure, stinking, fly-covered bullshit. Stop trying to make fetch happen. It's never going to happen.

The point of this monologue is that like Homer, Nolan is bound to his context. Just as the gods gave the ancients a map by which to explain their terrifyingly unknowable world, psychology provides us a map for our terrifyingly rationalized one. That does not mean we have traded superstition for a perfect account of ourselves. A psychological explanation can become just as convenient as a divine one if I use it to explain everything about my behavior except what I owe the people on the receiving end. Knowing why Odysseus needs to forget may help us understand him. It does not settle whether forgetting is something he is entitled to do. Theater lives in that liminal space where there still may be dragons. It's supposed to be uncomfortable. It's supposed to be alien. But it also needs to be familiar. Otherwise, it's too easy to dismiss. In this day and age, our dragons seem to reside more often on the inside. A person wants something. Another person stands in the way. Both have reasons. The reasons may be excellent. Somebody still has to decide what to do, and nobody gets to pause the performance until the gods reach a consensus on who should suffer the consequences. Raskolnikov’s theory about extraordinary people stepping beyond ordinary morality sounds considerably better before he has to live inside the consequences, just like our hero. Justifications don't bleed, but people do whether they're wearing swords and sandals or shirtsleeves. Crime and Punishment. If Dostoyevsky had angels step in and say "Put down the axe or you're gonna regret it!", I don't think that would land with a modern audience.

Justifications don't bleed, but people do whether they're wearing swords and sandals or shirtsleeves.

In an age of convenience defined by instant gratification where things go "viral" and you have to "do your own research" and money is considered speech and corporations are considered people and we whore ourselves out to influence each other with peer-to-peer product placements and get grifted by cryptocurrencies and hammered with microtransactions and force-fed everything-as-a-service subscription plans and get price gouged by algorithms on mayonnaise and insulin while billions of dollars a day are spent blowing shit up in foreign countries just so we can pay $10 a gallon for diesel what-in-the-ever-loving fuck am I supposed to get out of a story where Thunder Daddy is the ultimate authority when things get truly out of hand?! I can think of a person or three that are in desperate need of a good smiting. Well? We're waiting...What is left for the modern audience if not accountability? When is Godot going to show up??? This timeline is a farce, and it is full of enough menace to make Harold Pinter pause indefinitely. Hell is other people but most troubling, it is also ourselves. And so we turn inward. Accountability to ourselves. Accountability to each other. Accountability to our children. That is the ēthos of Nolan's Odysseus. That is the faithful betrayal.

People are complicated. Art exposes that complication. It is the artist's duty to show the conflict. That does not make every account equally credible, every action equally defensible, or every outcome somebody else’s fault. The gods above or the demons inside us are devices to tell that story. Neither is more right than the other. Both are designed to get you sweating and make you think. That’s the theater, baby. You bought the whole seat, but we’re gonna do our best to make sure you only get to sit comfortably in it for about twelve minutes.

04

The market made me do it

We have not outgrown the desire for a force that explains our choices while relieving us of responsibility for them. We've just given it new names.

The Market. The Algorithm. Progress. Tradition. Precedent. National Security.

It's the same appeal to fate. Don't fight it. It's just the way it is. It's inevitable. Sometimes these names identify real constraints. More often they just obfuscate the people who set the constraint and stand to benefit from everyone else accepting it.

I work in machine learning, artificial intelligence, and personalization. I am not looking at Silicon Valley from a distant monastery, wondering whether electricity was a mistake. I find this work fascinating. I also know that an optimization system requires something to optimize. A metric has to be selected. A tradeoff has to be accepted. Someone has to decide which harms appear on the dashboard and which get made into somebody else’s problem. “The algorithm did it” is an explanation that should begin an investigation, not end one.

“The algorithm did it” is an explanation that should begin an investigation, not end one.

Doctorow’s account of enshittification is useful here because it identifies a mechanism: platforms can exploit locked-in users and businesses when competition, regulation, labor, and interoperability cease to constrain them effectively. The decay has beneficiaries and enabling conditions. It is not simply the natural aging process of a website. Cory Doctorow, “Who broke the internet?”. Call it late-stage capitalism if you like, but now we're right back to Thunder Daddy. I want to know what the phrase is accusing somebody of doing. Which obligation was weakened? Who changed the incentive? Who made leaving harder? If we stop at the label, capitalism becomes another moody deity whose displeasure must be endured.

Regulatory capture makes the evasion particularly elegant: shape the rules, then appeal to the rules as evidence that your hands are tied. Social media gives the rest of us a smaller version of the same temptation. I can deplore the outrage machine while feeding it, then describe my participation as something the algorithm baited me into doing. The asymmetry of power is real. So is the convenience of that sentence. AI can be both a promised land and an apocalypse. The future will be so abundant that present objections look petty; or, the future will be so dangerous that extraordinary concentrations of power become necessary to manage it. Those are not equivalent forecasts, and serious safety research is not doomerism. But either promise becomes immediately suspicious when it asks us to surrender judgment to the people standing outside the big-ass wooden horse—the same people who keep trying to sack the city. Is the investment boom a bubble? How capable will the systems become? Important questions. Neither answers whether a particular deployment, labor decision, or transfer of power is justified now. “This is inevitable” is doing a lot of work in an argument about things people are actively building.

I am not suggesting that Nolan hid a position paper on any of these subjects inside the Cyclops’ cave. Nor should we confuse responsibility with the fantasy that sufficient virtue gives us control over everything. Sometimes the storm really is a storm. The gods are fickle, but so is the weather. Accountability means being specific about what people do and don't do—not simply finding someone to punish because uncertainty is intolerable. But if Nolan's brooding, prosaic Odysseus has a song to sing, it is to resist the temptation to describe the world we are helping make as a world in which we can only obey.

05

We are the people from the sea

War makes that temptation to obey lethal.

Iraq and Afghanistan did not invent moral ambiguity. The twentieth century was not a reliably tidy arrangement of good guys and bad guys, however efficiently the victory montages may sort the uniforms. But a war against a tactic or an ideology creates a particular problem: who can surrender on its behalf? What event establishes "Mission Accomplished"?

The post-9/11 disputes over “enemy combatants” were not merely arguments about vocabulary. They concerned detention, legal protections, and the power to decide whose status placed them outside the ordinary rules. The ICRC challenged the notion that those classifications could leave people in a legal vacuum. ICRC: challenges from the “War on Terror”. The rhetoric of necessity is familiar. We are defending civilization. The enemy does not honor our rules. We cannot afford to be sentimental. We will restore normality once we have secured the conditions that make normality possible. And in the meantime, people have to carry out the orders. Everyone else has to live with the consequences. Odysseus can tell the story as a series of dangers encountered on the way home. The people on those shores might tell it as the arrival of armed strangers who wanted their food and would not accept no. The captain’s account and the village’s account describe the same landing but they do not describe the same experience.

This is where the Sea Peoples revelation lands for me. The civilization-threatening force is no longer safely somewhere beyond the horizon. The men we have followed, the men whose hardships we understand, can themselves become that force. Nolan makes this identification part of his fictional treatment of the Bronze Age’s end; it is not an archaeological solution to the historical Sea Peoples question. The film’s ending. Destruction follows from people pursuing goals they consider necessary while treating the conditions of everybody else’s survival as negotiable. Self-destruction follows from absolving oneself of responsibility for one's own actions. However, “We are the Sea Peoples” does not mean that everyone is equally guilty. The person setting the course does not have the same freedom as the person chained to the oar. If collective responsibility makes those differences disappear, it becomes just another way for the powerful to spread the bill around and shirk the yoke of accountability. But neither can we quietly mean that it's purely the fault of those assholes over there. Not the billionaires, the politicians, the generals, the anonymous bootlickers in somebody else’s comment section, while I remain a morally intact spectator enjoying the show. I have spent these essays explaining why I recognize myself in Odysseus. It would be awfully convenient to stop recognizing him precisely when he becomes difficult to defend.

I want to believe I am adaptable. I want to believe I am useful. I want to believe that when I find a way around an obstacle, I know the difference between a pointless restriction and somebody else’s protection. Those are pleasant things to believe about myself but evidence of virtue, they are not. The mark of virtue is not in what I had to suffer on the way home. It is what happened to other people because of my actions while I was trying to get there—and whether I can bear to hear the account without attempting to immediately justify it.

We are the Sea Peoples. The gods did not build this Horse. I did. And so did you.
